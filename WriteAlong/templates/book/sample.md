# The Keeper’s Daughter

> The sea gives nothing back that it has not first changed.
>
> Breton proverb

Nobody on the island remembered a winter as long as the one in which Maren Ashby turned seventeen. The ferry stopped running in November, the telegraph wire came down in the first gale of December, and by the new year the only news from the mainland arrived in the beam of the lighthouse across the strait, blinking its steady, indifferent code.

Her father kept the light on their own side, as his father had done before him. He was a quiet man who measured his days in wicks and oil and the hours between dusk and dawn, and he did not believe in luck, good or bad. What he believed in was the logbook. Every night he wrote the wind, the weather and the ships that passed, in a hand so small and even that Maren, as a child, had thought it was printed.

“If it isn’t written down,” he liked to say, “it didn’t happen.”

She had always taken this as a joke. That winter she began to suspect he meant it.

---

The first strange entry appeared on the ninth of January. Maren found it by accident, turning back the pages to check the date of the last supply boat. Beneath the usual columns, in the same careful hand, her father had written a single line:

*Light seen on the salt road, 2 a.m. Moving north. No vessel.*

The salt road was what the islanders called the causeway that had once joined them to the mainland, before the sea took it a century ago. At the lowest spring tides you could still see the line of it, a pale scar of stones running out into the grey water and vanishing. Nothing could travel on it now. Nothing had for a hundred years.

She read the line three times, then closed the book and put it back exactly where she had found it, square to the edge of the desk, the way he liked it.

## The Second Entry

A week later there was another. Then two more, a night apart. Each recorded a light on the causeway, each a little closer to the island than the last. Her father said nothing at breakfast, ate his porridge, and went to sleep through the short grey day as he always did. But Maren noticed that he had begun to wind the clock in the lamp room twice each evening, and that he left the door to the gallery unlatched.

# Low Water

On the night of the spring tide, Maren did not go to bed. She sat on the stairs below the lamp room with a blanket around her shoulders and listened to the mechanism turn: the soft tick of the clockwork, the creak of the lens on its bed of mercury, the wind finding every gap in the old stone.

At a quarter to two she heard her father’s chair scrape back. She climbed the last few steps and found him at the gallery rail, the logbook open in one hand, a pencil in the other, staring out at the strait.

The tide was the lowest she had ever seen it. The causeway lay exposed for almost half its length, a black spine glistening under the moon. And on it, far out, where the stones disappeared into the water, there was a light.

It was not a lantern. It was too steady for that, and the wrong colour, a cold pale green like the phosphorescence that sometimes lit the wakes of boats in late summer. It moved slowly, without bobbing, as though whoever carried it walked on perfectly level ground.

“You’ve seen it before,” Maren said.

Her father did not turn around. “Every night this month.”

“What is it?”

He was quiet for a long time. Below them the sea drew back another inch, uncovering another stone.

“I don’t know,” he said at last. He looked down at the logbook, then held it out to her, and the pencil with it. “But someone should write it down.”
